To write content AI assistants can cite, answer one clear question per page, put the answer in the first few sentences, state facts in plain and specific language, and organise the rest with descriptive headings, lists and tables. Back claims with sources that can be checked, keep the same facts consistent across your site and make sure the text is readable by crawlers.

This guide turns those principles into concrete habits you can apply to new and existing pages.

## Why citation matters

When an assistant such as Perplexity, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Grok searches the web to answer a question, it reads a set of pages, writes an answer and often links to the sources it used. Those links are one of the few ways a brand gets credited, and sometimes clicked, inside an AI answer.

Even when an assistant answers from training data with no visible citation, the same qualities matter. Clear, consistent, widely repeated facts are easier for a model to learn and repeat accurately.

Citable content is not a trick. It is content that makes the assistant's job easier: finding the answer, understanding it and being confident it is correct. ## Lead with the answer

Many web pages bury the answer under an introduction, a story or a sales pitch. Assistants, like busy readers, look for the part that answers the question.

Use an answer-first pattern:

1. **First two or three sentences**: a direct, complete answer to the question in the heading.
2. **Next section**: the key details, conditions or exceptions.
3. **After that**: examples, steps, comparisons and background.

For example, a page titled "How long does it take to install a heat pump?" should say in its opening lines what affects the timeline and what a typical installation involves, rather than starting with the history of heating.

## One page, one clear question

Pages that try to answer everything tend to answer nothing well. Give each important question its own page, or its own clearly headed section on a larger page.

Signs a page is too broad:

- The title could describe several different articles.
- Readers have to scroll a long way to find the specific answer they came for.
- The page targets several unrelated searches at once.

Focused pages are easier to cite, because the assistant can link to a source that clearly matches the question it is answering.

## Write facts that can be lifted cleanly

An assistant is more likely to use a sentence that makes sense on its own. Compare these two versions:

| Harder to cite | Easier to cite |
| --- | --- |
| "As mentioned above, it depends, but we can usually help." | "We install heat pumps for homes in the city and surrounding towns, and most jobs are scheduled within a few weeks of a site survey." |
| "Our pricing is flexible to suit everyone." | "Pricing is per month, with a lower-cost plan for a single user and a higher plan for small teams." |
| "We are the leading solution in the space." | "The software is designed for independent accounting firms that manage payroll for small businesses." |

Good citable sentences:

- Name the subject instead of relying on "it" or "this".
- Include the specific detail: what, who, where, how much or how long.
- Avoid vague superlatives such as "best" or "leading" unless you can show why.
- Stay accurate when read out of context.

## Structure pages for skimming

Clear structure helps assistants identify which part of a page answers which question.

- **Descriptive headings.** Write headings that state the topic or question, such as "What the starter plan includes", not "More info".
- **Short paragraphs.** One idea per paragraph.
- **Lists for steps and options.** Numbered lists for sequences, bullets for options.
- **Tables for comparisons.** Features, plans, specifications and differences are easier to read in a table.
- **FAQ sections.** Short questions with short answers, written in plain text.

Add structured data where it fits, such as FAQ, Product, Organization or Article markup, and make sure it matches what the page actually says. The technical side is covered in [the AI-ready website checklist](/blog/ai-ready-website-checklist).

## Back up claims with verifiable sources

Assistants and the people reading their answers both look for signs that information is reliable.

- Link to primary sources, such as official documentation, regulations or standards, when you reference them.
- Show who wrote the content and why they are qualified to write it.
- Show when the page was last updated, and actually keep it up to date.
- Separate facts from opinion. If something is your view, say so.

Never invent figures, quotes or study results to make a page sound more authoritative. Beyond the trust problem, false claims that get repeated by an assistant can be hard to correct later.

## Keep your facts consistent everywhere

If your website, social profiles, directory listings and press coverage disagree about what you do, where you operate or what you charge, an assistant has to choose which version to believe. Sometimes it chooses the wrong one.

A simple consistency check:

1. Write a short fact sheet: your name, what you sell, who it is for, locations, pricing model and key differences.
2. Compare it with your homepage, about page, pricing page and main product pages.
3. Compare it with your main third-party profiles and listings.
4. Fix any mismatch, starting with your own site.

When an assistant does get your facts wrong, our guide on [what to do when AI gets your brand wrong](/blog/ai-misinformation-about-your-brand) walks through how to find the cause.

## Improve the pages you already have

You do not need to start from scratch. Existing pages that already rank or attract visitors are often the quickest wins, because search engines and assistants can already find them.

Start with the pages that matter most to buyers: your main service or product pages, your pricing page and any guides that answer common questions. For each one:

- Rewrite the opening so it answers the page's main question directly.
- Replace vague headings with descriptive ones.
- Split long paragraphs and turn buried comparisons into tables.
- Remove outdated details and update the last-updated date only when you have made real changes.

Make one round of changes, then re-check how assistants answer the related questions before moving on to the next batch.

## A checklist for each page

Before you publish or update a page, run through this list:

1. Does the title state the question or topic clearly?
2. Is there a direct answer in the first two or three sentences?
3. Does each heading describe what its section covers?
4. Are key facts written as specific, self-contained sentences?
5. Are comparisons and options shown in tables or lists?
6. Are claims backed by sources a reader could check?
7. Is the author and the last-updated date visible?
8. Do the facts match your other pages and profiles?
9. Is the main text present in the HTML and not hidden behind scripts, tabs or images?
10. Does the page link to related pages on your site?

## Key takeaways

- Answer the question first, in plain and specific language.
- Give each important question its own focused page or section.
- Write sentences that stay accurate when quoted on their own.
- Use descriptive headings, lists, tables and short FAQs.
- Back claims with verifiable sources and keep your facts consistent across the web.
