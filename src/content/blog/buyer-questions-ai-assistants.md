Buyers ask AI assistants for recommendations in full, conversational questions that describe their situation, such as "What is the best accounting software for a five-person agency that invoices in two currencies?" They then refine the answer with follow-ups, compare a shortlist and ask about price and fit. To be part of those answers, you need to understand the question types buyers use at each stage and publish clear content that answers them.

This guide breaks down those question types, shows how they differ from search keywords, and explains how to turn them into a practical prompt list for your brand.

## How AI questions differ from search keywords

People have long typed short phrases into search engines, such as "accounting software small business". With an AI assistant, many buyers write the way they would talk to a knowledgeable friend.

| Search habit | AI assistant habit |
| --- | --- |
| Short keyword phrases | Full sentences and paragraphs |
| One query, then scan a list of links | A conversation with several follow-ups |
| Context is implied | Context is stated: budget, size, location, constraints |
| Buyer compares options across many tabs | Assistant is asked to compare and summarise |
| Result is a ranked list of pages | Result is a written answer that may name a few brands |

The practical effect is that buyers often receive a short list of named options instead of ten links. If your brand is not in that short list, the buyer may never visit your site. This is also why a strong Google position does not guarantee a mention, a gap we cover in [why ranking on Google does not mean AI mentions you](/blog/google-rank-vs-ai-answers).

## The stages of an AI-assisted buying journey

Buyer questions tend to follow the same broad stages as any purchase, but each stage has its own style of prompt.

### 1. Problem framing

The buyer is not yet looking for a product. They want to understand the problem.

- "Why do my customers keep cancelling after the first month?"
- "How do small restaurants usually handle staff scheduling?"

Brands rarely get named here, but the assistant may describe the type of solution, which shapes what the buyer searches for next.

### 2. Category discovery

The buyer knows the kind of solution they need and asks for options.

- "What are the best tools for scheduling restaurant staff?"
- "Which project management apps work well for small creative teams?"

This is where most brand mentions first appear, and where being absent hurts most.

### 3. Requirements and fit

The buyer adds constraints to narrow the list.

- "Which of these work offline and cost less than a typical per-seat plan?"
- "I need something that integrates with my existing accounting tool and supports two languages. What do you recommend?"

Assistants match options to stated constraints, so clear, specific information about your features, integrations and limits matters.

### 4. Comparison

The buyer asks the assistant to weigh up named options.

- "Compare [Brand A] and [Brand B] for a team of ten."
- "What are the pros and cons of [your brand]?"

### 5. Validation

Before buying, the buyer checks for risks.

- "Is [your brand] reliable? What do users complain about?"
- "Does [your brand] have a free trial or a refund policy?"

Errors at this stage are costly. If an assistant states the wrong refund policy or an outdated price, the buyer may leave. Our guide on [when AI gets your brand wrong](/blog/ai-misinformation-about-your-brand) explains how to spot and fix these errors.

## Common prompt patterns buyers use

Across categories, a handful of patterns come up again and again. Knowing them makes it easier to build a realistic test set.

- **"Best X for Y."** "Best CRM for a solo consultant."
- **"Alternatives to X."** "What are cheaper alternatives to [well-known brand]?"
- **"X vs Y."** "[Brand A] vs [Brand B] for a growing online shop."
- **"Is X worth it?"** "Is [your brand] worth it for a small team?"
- **"How do I..."** "How do I automate invoice reminders?" The answer may recommend a tool.
- **"I am a [role] at a [type of company]..."** A persona-led question with detailed context.
- **"Near me" or location-based.** "Good family dentists in [city] that open on Saturdays."

Many buyers also ask the assistant to explain its reasoning or to give sources, especially on engines that cite pages, such as Perplexity.

## How context changes the answer

Because buyers include context, the same core question can produce different recommendations. For example, a buyer asking for "the best email marketing tool" might get a different short list from one asking for "the best email marketing tool for a nonprofit with a small budget and no technical staff".

Context that commonly changes answers includes:

- Company size or number of users
- Budget or pricing model preferences
- Industry or use case
- Location and language
- Existing tools that need to integrate
- Technical skill of the buyer

When you build your prompt list, include several versions of each important question with different context. Otherwise you may see a mention in one framing and assume you are visible everywhere.

## How AI assistants decide what to recommend

The general mechanism is well understood even if the details differ between engines and change over time. An assistant draws on what its model learned in training and, for many questions, on pages it retrieves from the web. It then writes an answer that tries to match the buyer's stated needs.

Brands that are described clearly and consistently across many credible sources are commonly observed to be mentioned more often. Specific, factual pages that state who a product is for, what it costs and how it compares give an assistant more to work with than vague marketing copy. ## Build your own buyer prompt list

A good prompt list reflects how your actual buyers talk. Use this checklist to build one.

1. **Collect real questions.** Review sales call notes, demo requests, support tickets, live chat logs and site search terms.
2. **Check community questions.** Look at forums, review sites and social threads in your category for how people phrase their needs.
3. **Map each question to a stage.** Problem framing, discovery, requirements, comparison or validation.
4. **Rewrite as full prompts.** Add the context a real buyer would give: size, budget, location, constraints.
5. **Create variations.** Two or three phrasings per core question, including one that names a competitor.
6. **Prioritise.** Focus first on discovery and comparison prompts, where brands are most often named.
7. **Test across engines.** Run the list in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok and Perplexity, and record who is mentioned.
8. **Review regularly.** Update the list when you launch products, enter new markets or hear new questions from buyers.

As an example, a regional bakery supplier might end up with prompts such as "Where can a small cafe in [region] buy wholesale pastries with next-day delivery?" and "Compare wholesale bakery suppliers in [region] for a cafe that needs vegan options."

## Turn buyer questions into content

Once you know the questions, make sure your site answers them directly.

- **Discovery questions:** category pages that state plainly what you offer and who it is for.
- **Requirements questions:** feature, integration and pricing pages with specific, current details.
- **Comparison questions:** honest comparison pages that explain where you fit and where another option may suit better.
- **Validation questions:** clear pages on trials, refunds, security, support and reliability.
- **How-to questions:** practical guides that solve the problem and mention your product where it helps.

Write in plain sentences, lead with the answer and keep facts consistent across your site and third-party profiles. Keeping a public pricing page current, such as a dedicated [pricing](/pricing) page, also helps assistants answer cost questions correctly.

## Key takeaways

- Buyers ask AI assistants full, context-rich questions and refine them with follow-ups.
- Questions follow stages: problem framing, discovery, requirements, comparison and validation.
- A handful of patterns, such as "best X for Y", "alternatives to X" and "X vs Y", cover many real prompts.
- Context like size, budget and location changes which brands are recommended, so test several versions of each question.
- Build your prompt list from real buyer language, then publish clear pages that answer each stage.
