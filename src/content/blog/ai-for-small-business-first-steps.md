The best first steps with AI for a small business are to choose one or two repetitive tasks, try a general AI assistant on them with clear rules about review and data, and keep only what saves real time. At the same time, check how AI assistants describe your business to potential customers, because many buyers now ask them for recommendations before they search.

You do not need a large budget, a data team or a long strategy document to begin. This guide sets out a practical path, with examples clearly marked as examples.

## Start with the problem, not the tool

It is easy to sign up for several AI tools and then wonder what to do with them. Start the other way round: list the tasks that take up time every week and that follow a predictable pattern.

Good candidates usually share three traits:

- **Repetitive**: the task happens often.
- **Text-heavy**: it involves writing, reading, summarising or sorting information.
- **Easy to check**: a person can quickly tell whether the output is right.

Tasks that are rare, high-risk or hard to check, such as legal advice or final financial decisions, are poor places to start.

## Everyday tasks worth trying first

Here are common starting points. Each is an example of where a general assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini can help, not a promise of a specific result.

| Task | How AI can help | What a person still does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer emails | Draft replies to common questions | Check facts, tone and anything about money or commitments |
| Meeting and call notes | Summarise notes into actions | Confirm the actions and owners |
| Website and product copy | Suggest first drafts and alternatives | Edit for accuracy and brand voice |
| Social media posts | Turn one announcement into several formats | Approve and schedule |
| Internal documents | Turn rough notes into a clear checklist or procedure | Make sure it matches how work is really done |
| Research | Explain unfamiliar topics or summarise long documents | Verify important points against original sources |

For example, a small plumbing company might use an assistant to turn a technician's rough job notes into a tidy summary for the customer, with the office manager checking each one before it is sent.

## Set simple rules before you scale

A short set of rules prevents most problems. Write them down and share them with the team.

1. **Review everything.** AI output is a draft. A person checks it before it reaches a customer.
2. **Protect sensitive data.** Do not paste customer personal details, passwords, payment information or confidential contracts into a tool unless its terms and your obligations clearly allow it.
3. **Check facts.** Assistants can produce confident but wrong statements. Verify names, figures, dates and legal or medical points.
4. **Be open where it matters.** If AI plays a significant role in something a customer relies on, consider telling them.
5. **Keep a record.** Note which tools you use and for what, so you can review them later.

Read the data and privacy settings of any tool you adopt. Many providers offer business plans with different data handling from their free versions, so compare them before deciding.

## Run a small, time-boxed trial

Treat your first use of AI as an experiment.

1. Pick one task and one tool.
2. Agree how you will judge success, for example time saved per week or fewer back-and-forth emails.
3. Use it consistently for a few weeks.
4. Ask the people doing the work what helped and what did not.
5. Decide: keep it, change how you use it or drop it.

For example, a small accounting practice might trial an assistant only for drafting reminder emails to clients about upcoming deadlines. After a few weeks, the team can compare how long the emails took to prepare before and during the trial, and whether any drafts needed major corrections.

This avoids spending money on tools nobody uses, and it gives you real experience before committing to anything larger.

## Do not forget the other side: how buyers use AI

Using AI inside your business is only half the picture. Your customers are using it too.

Many people now ask assistants questions such as "who is a reliable electrician near me" or "what is a good bookkeeping service for a small cafe". The assistant writes an answer and usually names only a few businesses. If yours is not among them, the customer may never see you.

This is a different challenge from ranking on Google. A business can appear on the first page of search results and still be missing from AI answers. ## Quick checks to see how AI describes your business

You can do a basic check yourself in an afternoon.

1. Write down a handful of questions a new customer might ask, without naming your business.
2. Ask each question in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok and Perplexity.
3. Note whether your business is mentioned and which competitors appear instead.
4. Ask each assistant directly about your business and check the details: services, location, opening hours and pricing.
5. Save the answers so you can compare them later.

If the details are wrong or missing, the first places to look are your own website and your main listings and review profiles.

## Simple fixes that help both search and AI

Most of the work that helps AI assistants understand your business is basic good practice:

- **Keep your website facts clear.** State what you do, where you work and how to contact you in plain text on your main pages.
- **Answer common questions.** A simple FAQ page that answers real customer questions directly is useful to people and machines.
- **Keep listings consistent.** Make sure your name, address, phone number and services match across directories and profiles.
- **Encourage honest reviews.** Reviews that describe what you actually did help assistants understand your strengths.
- **Let crawlers read your site.** Make sure your site does not accidentally block the crawlers used by AI assistants.

The technical side is covered in more depth in [the AI-ready website checklist](/blog/ai-ready-website-checklist).

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Buying several tools at once before testing one properly.
- Publishing AI-written content without checking it for accuracy.
- Pasting confidential information into tools without reading their data terms.
- Assuming a good Google ranking means AI assistants know about you.
- Treating AI as a one-off project instead of something to review regularly.

## Key takeaways

- Start with one repetitive, easy-to-check task and a single tool.
- Set clear rules on review, data and fact-checking before expanding.
- Run short trials and keep only what saves real time.
- Check how AI assistants describe your business to potential customers.
- Clear website facts, consistent listings and honest reviews help both search and AI.
