To track competitors in AI answers, choose a fixed set of buyer prompts, run them regularly in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok and Perplexity, and record which brands each answer mentions, in what order, how they are described and which sources are cited. Comparing those records over time shows where competitors lead, where you lead and what content or sources explain the difference.

This guide sets out a repeatable method you can run in a spreadsheet, the metrics worth recording, and how to turn what you find into action.

## Why competitor tracking in AI answers is different

On a search results page, you can see every competitor's position at a glance. In an AI answer, a buyer may see only a short list of names, or a single recommendation, with a sentence or two about each. Being left out of that list, or being described less favourably than a competitor, is easy to miss unless you look on purpose.

A few things make this kind of tracking its own discipline:

- **Answers vary by engine.** Each assistant has different training data and retrieval behaviour, so a competitor can dominate one and be absent from another.
- **Answers vary by wording.** Small changes in a prompt can change which brands appear.
- **Search rank is not a proxy.** A competitor you outrank on Google may still be named more often by assistants. We explain why in [why ranking on Google does not mean AI mentions you](/blog/google-rank-vs-ai-answers).
- **Descriptions matter as much as mentions.** How a competitor is framed, for example as the "best for small teams" option, shapes buyer choices.

## Step 1: Define your competitor set

Start with a list, then let the assistants extend it.

1. **Known competitors.** The brands you lose deals to and the ones buyers mention on sales calls.
2. **Category leaders.** Well-known brands in your space, even if they serve a different segment.
3. **Unexpected names.** Any brand that appears in assistant answers for your prompts but is not on your list. Add these as you find them.

Keep the list manageable. For many businesses, a handful of direct competitors plus a few category leaders is enough to see clear patterns.

## Step 2: Build a prompt set that reflects real buyers

Your prompts decide what you learn. Use the questions buyers actually ask, at each stage of their decision. Our guide on [how buyers ask AI assistants for recommendations](/blog/buyer-questions-ai-assistants) covers the common patterns in depth.

A balanced competitor prompt set usually includes:

- **Category prompts:** "What are the best [category] tools for [use case]?"
- **Constraint prompts:** "Which [category] options work for a team of ten on a tight budget?"
- **Alternative prompts:** "What are the alternatives to [competitor]?"
- **Head-to-head prompts:** "[Your brand] vs [competitor] for [use case]."
- **Location prompts,** if relevant: "Best [service] in [city]."

Write each prompt once, then keep its wording fixed. Changing wording between checks makes trends impossible to read.

## Step 3: Record the right data for each answer

For every prompt and every engine, capture the same fields. A simple tracking table might look like this:

| Field | What to record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prompt | The exact wording | Keeps checks comparable |
| Engine | ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok or Perplexity | Shows engine-specific gaps |
| Date | When the answer was collected | Lets you see trends and model changes |
| Brands mentioned | Every brand named, in order | Basis for share of voice and position |
| Your position | First, second, later or absent | Earlier mentions are more prominent to the reader |
| Description | How each brand is framed | Reveals positioning and errors |
| Sources cited | URLs the engine used, where shown | Points to the pages driving the answer |
| Your Google rank | Your position for the closest search query | Shows where search and AI diverge |

Use a fresh session for each prompt, and save the full answer text, not just a summary. You will often want to reread it later.

## Step 4: Turn raw answers into comparable metrics

Once you have several rounds of data, a few simple measures make competitors easy to compare.

- **Mention rate:** the share of prompts in which each brand appears, per engine and overall.
- **Average position:** how early each brand tends to be named when it does appear.
- **First-mention count:** how often each brand is named first.
- **Engine coverage:** in how many of the five engines a brand appears for a given prompt.
- **Sentiment and framing:** whether each brand is described positively, neutrally or with caveats.

Together these give a competitor version of AI share of voice. Our guide on [how to measure your brand's share of voice in AI answers](/blog/measure-ai-share-of-voice) explains the calculation and common pitfalls.

## Step 5: Find out why a competitor is ahead

Numbers show where you are behind. The answer text and sources show why. For each prompt where a competitor leads, look for patterns.

- **Which sources are cited?** If an engine repeatedly cites a review site, a comparison article or a directory that features a competitor but not you, that source is a likely lever.
- **What language is used?** If the assistant calls a competitor "the best option for agencies", look at whether that phrase, or the idea behind it, appears on their site and in third-party coverage.
- **What facts are stated?** Assistants often repeat specific details: pricing tiers, integrations, supported regions. A competitor with clear, consistent facts across the web gives assistants more to work with.
- **What is missing about you?** If your brand is absent, check whether your own pages state clearly who you serve and what you offer.

For example, a project management tool might find that a rival is named first for "best tool for creative agencies" in several engines, and that the cited sources are agency-focused comparison articles where the rival is reviewed and it is not. That points to a specific outreach and content task rather than a vague goal.

## Step 6: Act on the gaps

Turn each finding into a concrete task. Typical actions include:

1. **Publish missing pages.** Use-case pages, honest comparison pages and clear pricing pages for segments where competitors lead.
2. **Clarify your positioning.** State plainly who you are best for, in words buyers use.
3. **Earn coverage in cited sources.** Reach out to the publications, directories and review sites that engines cite for your category.
4. **Correct errors.** If assistants describe you inaccurately next to competitors, fix the source pages and listings.
5. **Re-check after changes.** Run the same prompts again to see whether the answers moved.

Keep a simple log linking each action to the prompts it was meant to affect, so you can judge what worked.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Tracking too many prompts at first.** Start with a focused set you can review properly, then expand.
- **Changing prompts between rounds.** It breaks comparability.
- **Checking one engine only.** Visibility in one assistant says little about the others.
- **Reading a single answer as a trend.** Answers can vary between runs, so look at patterns across several prompts and rounds.
- **Copying competitors' wording.** Learn from how they are described, but describe your own strengths accurately.

## How often to track

A monthly cadence suits many businesses. Add extra checks after your own launches or pricing changes, after a competitor's major announcement, and when an assistant releases a new model, since answers can shift noticeably after an update. Keep the prompt list and recording method the same so each round can be compared with the last.

## Key takeaways

- Define a competitor set that includes unexpected brands the assistants mention.
- Use a fixed list of real buyer prompts and run it across all five major engines.
- Record mentions, position, framing, sources and your Google rank for every answer.
- Convert the data into mention rate, average position and engine coverage to compare brands fairly.
- Use cited sources and answer language to explain why competitors lead, then act on specific gaps.
